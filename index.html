What the colours mean
  • On the body: red is oxygenated blood and blue is deoxygenated blood. That is the convention every diagram uses. Real blood is never blue: deoxygenated blood is dark red.
  • In the text: crimson words are about the heart; amber words are about the blood vessels and the routes the blood takes; violet words are about the blood itself; teal words are about exercise, measuring the heart and coronary heart disease.
  • A box with a red border is the mistake students most often make there. A small S badge means Supplement (Paper 4 only). “Beyond the syllabus” is worth knowing but not named in 0610.
What is simplified here
  • The body is a real anatomical drawing, so every vessel and organ is where it is in the body. It is still a simplified drawing: only the larger vessels are shown, and the capillaries that join them in every organ are too small to draw.
  • The small intestine is drawn faintly, under the vessels. Seen from the front, a real one would hide the aorta and the vena cava behind it.
  • The moving dots show the direction the blood flows, away from the heart in the arteries and towards it in the veins. Real blood cells are far too small to see at this scale, and far more numerous.
  • The magnified heart is drawn as every exam diagram draws it: cut from the front, so its right side is on your left. The aorta and the pulmonary artery leave the top side by side; in the body the pulmonary artery twists round the front of the aorta. Each atrioventricular valve is drawn as two flaps; the one on the right side of the heart really has three.
  • In the animations, cells and molecules are drawn much larger and far fewer than in real life, and every process is slowed down so each step can be read.
  • The fish and mammal circuits are circuit diagrams, not drawings of real hearts: there the aorta leaves the side of the left ventricle, and one vena cava is drawn.
  • In the coronary artery, the blood flow falls as the fatty deposit grows. At rest a real artery keeps its flow until it is badly narrowed; the shortage shows first during exercise.
Where the content and the pictures come from
  • The definitions are the 2026–28 syllabus wording. Where the slides use an older wording it is given in brackets.
  • The stations follow Dr Mompel's own Year 10 lessons on Topic 9, and most of the pictures are the ones shown in those lessons.
  • The body on the left is Mariana Ruiz Villarreal's Circulatory System (LadyofHats, Wikimedia Commons, public domain), drawn from Gray's Anatomy and the Sobotta atlas — the same artist as the Digestion Lab's body. Its small intestine is hers too, from her Digestive system (with Jmarchn, public domain). The flowing blood follows the centre line of every vessel she drew; the magnified heart is drawn for this lab.
  • The 3D heart is a real human heart: the HuBMAP Human Reference Atlas (Browne and Schlehlein 2024), made from the Visible Human Male of the US National Library of Medicine, CC BY 4.0. Its moving valve cusps, tendons and blood paths are added for this lab.
